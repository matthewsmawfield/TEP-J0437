2. Synchronization Holonomy in Pulsar Scintillation

2.1 The Closure Delay Observable

When pulsar signals traverse the interstellar medium, scattering from plasma inhomogeneities creates multiple propagation paths. These paths interfere in the observer's dynamic spectrum, producing characteristic patterns that encode information about the scattering geometry. In standard scintillation theory, each scattered propagation path is assigned a scalar delay \(\tau_i\). Differential delays are differences of these scalars, so the closure sum around any triplet vanishes identically by arithmetic—a property termed synchronization consistency.

The closure residual \(C_{ijk}\) tests this additive representation. Each cross-term \(\hat{\tau}_{ab}\) is independently measured from the secondary-spectrum peak between scattered paths \(a\) and \(b\) via sub-pixel interpolation. For a triplet of scintillation arclets indexed by \(i, j, k\) forming a closed loop, the closure residual is:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:closure_residual} C_{ijk} = \hat{\tau}_{ij} + \hat{\tau}_{jk} + \hat{\tau}_{ki} \end{equation}

Under the additive-delay null hypothesis, there exists a scalar assignment \(\tau_i\) such that \(\hat{\tau}_{ab} = \tau_b - \tau_a\) within measurement noise, implying \(C_{ijk} \equiv 0\). The closure construction cancels common-mode contributions—including standard relativistic effects that apply equally to all scattered paths—by design, isolating only path-dependent contributions that violate the additive representation. TEP predicts a non-zero synchronization holonomy \(H_{\rm resid}^{(\tau)}\), defined as the GR-subtracted loop integral of the synchronization connection \(\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)} = \tilde{\sigma}^{(\tau)} - \sigma_{\rm GR}^{(\tau)}\) around the closed path (Jakarta, Paper 0). This holonomy—a geometric phase for clocks—arises because the disformal sector \(B(\phi) \neq 0\) of the matter metric \(\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}\) generates a non-exact contribution to the synchronization connection when \(u \cdot \nabla\phi \neq 0\). The observed closure delay relates to the holonomy magnitude as:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:holonomy_magnitude} M_{\rm cl} = |\langle \tau_{\text{closure}} \rangle_{\text{aligned}}| \end{equation}

where \(\langle \cdot \rangle_{\text{aligned}}\) denotes the Stokes-aligned average accounting for the relative orientation of the path triplet and the observer's velocity (see Section 4.7 for the alignment algorithm specification). The alignment ensures that delays correlate with geometric orientation as predicted by TEP.

The primary detection statistic is the Phase Closure \(\psi\), constructed from the geometric phase of the closure delay triplet. The absolute magnitude \(M_{\rm cl}\) is susceptible to Rice-distribution noise-floor bias: for zero-mean Gaussian noise with standard deviation \(\sigma\), the expectation of the absolute value is \(\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}\). For J0437-4715 the per-epoch noise floor is quantified at \(E[M_{\rm cl}] = 6.810\) ns, which saturates the raw unsigned mean (\(8.100 \pm 0.102\) ns), leaving a noise-subtracted excess of \(1.290 \pm 0.102\) ns (\(t = 12.7\sigma\)). The Phase Closure \(\psi\) is an odd-parity metric that cancels to zero in the absence of path-dependent proper time and is immune to this bias:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:phase_closure} \psi_{ijk} = \operatorname{Arg}\!\left[\exp\!\left(i\left(\phi_{ij} + \phi_{jk} - \phi_{ik}\right)\right)\right] \end{equation}

where \(\phi_{ab}\) is the phase of the cross-term between scattered paths \(a\) and \(b\), measured directly from the secondary spectrum. The minus sign on \(\phi_{ik}\) reflects the actual geometry: the three independently measured cross-terms are \((i,j)\), \((j,k)\) and the direct difference \((i,k)\); their closure is \(\phi_{ij} + \phi_{jk} - \phi_{ik}\). For a sample of \(N\) triplet measurements, the circular mean is:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:phase_closure_mean} \bar{\psi} = \operatorname{Arg}\!\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ijk} \exp\!\left(i\psi_{ijk}\right)\right] \end{equation}

TEP predicts \(\bar{\psi} \neq 0\) while the additive-delay null hypothesis predicts \(\bar{\psi} = 0\).

Why ordinary ray phases close but TEP synchronization transport need not. If every measured cross-term is factorizable as \(Q_{ab} = X_a X_b^*\), then \(\arg(Q_{ij}Q_{jk}Q_{ki}) = 0\) identically. This is precisely the scalar-delay null tested here: each ray carries a scalar accumulated phase \(\Theta_a\), and the closure \((\Theta_i - \Theta_j) + (\Theta_j - \Theta_k) + (\Theta_k - \Theta_i) = 0\) vanishes regardless of path dependence, because endpoint phases are state functions. TEP modifies the operational comparison itself: the pairwise observable is the transport of synchronization between the path-defined events associated with rays \(a\) and \(b\),

\begin{equation} \label{eq:tep_pairwise} \varphi_{ab}^{\rm TEP} = \varphi_b - \varphi_a + \int_{\Gamma_{ab}} \omega_{\rm eff}(x)\,\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)} , \end{equation}

where \(\Gamma_{ab}\) is the comparison path in spacetime induced by the scattering geometry, \(\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)}\) is the time-valued residual synchronization transport (units of seconds), and \(\omega_{\rm eff}(x)\) is the effective angular frequency that converts synchronization transport to phase. The time-valued residual holonomy is:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:h_resid_tau} H_{\rm resid}^{(\tau)}(C) \equiv \oint_C \Delta\sigma^{(\tau)} \qquad [\mathrm{s}] , \end{equation}

and the phase closure is:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:tep_closure} \psi_{ijk} = \operatorname{Arg}\!\exp\!\left[i\oint_C \omega_{\rm eff}(x)\,\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)}\right] . \end{equation}

Only when \(\omega_{\rm eff}\) is common to all three legs may this be reduced to \(\psi_{ijk} = \operatorname{wrap}\!\left[\omega_{\rm eff}\,H_{\rm resid}^{(\tau)}(C_{ijk})\right]\). The effective frequency \(\omega_{\rm eff}\) is determined by the secondary-spectrum measurement channel: it is the angular frequency at which the cross-term interference pattern is sampled, set by the observing band centre and the cross-spectrum extraction procedure. It is not simply the carrier frequency; the full identification requires an explicit electromagnetic transfer derivation (Appendix A.4). A direct empirical test of the phase–time mapping is available through the subband derivative:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:omega_eff_test} \frac{d\psi_{\rm unwrap}}{d\omega_{\rm eff}} = H_{\rm resid}^{(\tau)} , \end{equation}

valid for an unwrapped phase and a frequency-independent residual over the fitted subband. If frequency dependence is allowed, \(d\psi_{\rm unwrap}/d\omega = H + \omega\,dH/d\omega\). The derivative connects the phase and delay channels and is testable across observing subbands. The null \(\psi = 0\) is recovered whenever \(\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)}\) is exact, including the conformal-only sector, whereas the disformal non-exact sector permits \(\psi \neq 0\). The TEP measurement operator modifies each cross-term as:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:tep_measurement} Q_{ab}^{\rm TEP} = X_a\,X_b^*\,\exp\!\left[i\int_{\Gamma_{ab}} \omega_{\rm eff}(x)\,\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)}\right] . \end{equation}

Within the TEP measurement model, synchronization transport contributes an edge-dependent phase, so the three pairwise terms need not factorize into endpoint phases and their closure can be non-zero. Appendix A.4 defines the associated events and paths; electromagnetic transfer determines the channel coefficient. The closure product is then \(Q_{ij}^{\rm TEP} Q_{jk}^{\rm TEP} Q_{ki}^{\rm TEP} = |X_i X_j X_k|^2 \exp(i \oint_C \omega_{\rm eff}\,\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)})\), so \(\psi_{ijk} = \operatorname{Arg}\exp(i \oint_C \omega_{\rm eff}\,\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)})\). The measured cross-term is non-factorizable under TEP because the synchronization transport along each edge is an independent path integral, not a difference of state functions. The full identification of the three measured cross-terms with the edges of a genuine closed spacetime loop, including the event and path definitions, is given in Appendix A.4.

2.1.1 Statistical Motivation for Phase Closure

The choice of \(\psi\) as the primary detection statistic follows from the symmetry properties of the cross-term closure phase, independent of any specific theoretical framework. Define the complex closure product:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:closure_product} \mathcal{B}_{ijk} = Q_{ij}\,Q_{jk}\,Q_{ik}^{*}, \qquad \psi_{ijk} = \arg \mathcal{B}_{ijk} . \end{equation}

Under scalar factorization (\(Q_{ab} = X_a X_b^*\)), \(\mathcal{B}_{ijk} = |X_i X_j X_k|^2\), and therefore \(\psi_{ijk} = 0\). Measurement and extraction errors broaden the observed distribution around the scalar-null direction. TEP synchronization curvature adds a nonfactorizable link phase, shifting the circular mean away from zero. The folded closure magnitude \(M_{\rm cl} = \langle |C_{ijk}| \rangle\), by contrast, is strictly positive under any noise model—it measures the mean absolute deviation, not a physical holonomy. This is the Rice-distribution bias: \(M_{\rm cl} > 0\) even when no signal is present.

The Phase Closure \(\psi\) is therefore the appropriate zero-centered test statistic: it cancels to zero for additive delays regardless of noise level, while any physical mechanism producing correlated delay-Doppler structure (including but not limited to TEP) yields \(\langle \psi \rangle \neq 0\). The Rayleigh Z = 59.21 (p = 1.39 × 10^{-13}) detection of non-zero \(\psi\) reported in this work constitutes a model-independent rejection of the additive-delay null hypothesis. The interpretation of this rejection as synchronization holonomy follows from the TEP framework, but the statistical significance of the rejection itself does not depend on TEP.

2.2 TEP Predictions for Closure Delays

The TEP framework—a two-metric scalar-tensor theory introduced in Paper 0—makes a central prediction that directly contrasts with the standard additive-delay model: the synchronization holonomy must be non-zero. The theory posits a disformal coupling between a scalar field $\phi$ and the matter metric $\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}$ (Bekenstein 1993; Koivisto & Zumalacárregui 2012):

\begin{equation} \label{eq:disformal_metric} \tilde{g}_{\mu\nu} = A^2(\phi) g_{\mu\nu} + B(\phi) \nabla_\mu\phi \nabla_\nu\phi \end{equation}

where $g_{\mu\nu}$ is the Einstein metric governing gravitational dynamics, $A(\phi)$ is the conformal coupling function, and $B(\phi)$ is the disformal coupling parameter. The observational observable is not a raw one-way speed or the integral of a scalar proper-time differential. Following the Jakarta construction (Paper 0, Section 3.3 and Appendix A3), let $\tilde{\sigma}^{(\tau)}$ denote the matter-frame synchronization transport one-form induced by $\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}$ and the chosen clock congruence, and let $\sigma_{\rm GR}^{(\tau)}$ denote the corresponding GR one-form including Sagnac, gravitomagnetic/Lense–Thirring, Shapiro delay, gravitational redshift, station motion, clock-scale realization, and reference-frame corrections. The residual synchronization connection is:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:residual_sigma} \Delta\sigma^{(\tau)} \equiv \tilde{\sigma}^{(\tau)} - \sigma_{\rm GR}^{(\tau)} \end{equation}

and the time-valued residual holonomy around a closed loop $C = \partial\Sigma$ is:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:holonomy_resid} H_{\rm resid}^{(\tau)}(C) = \oint_C \Delta\sigma^{(\tau)} = \iint_\Sigma d(\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)}) \end{equation}

This quantity is invariant under admissible synchronization re-gaugings because both $\tilde{\sigma}^{(\tau)}$ and $\sigma_{\rm GR}^{(\tau)}$ shift by the same exact one-form, leaving $\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)}$ unchanged. In the purely conformal subclass ($B = 0$), the scalar contribution to clock-rate transport is generated by the exact one-form $\omega^{(A)} = d\ln A$, so $\oint_C d\ln A = 0$ for any closed loop in a simply connected region. A leading-order nonzero $H_{\rm resid}^{(\tau)}$ requires residual synchronization curvature $d(\Delta\sigma^{(\tau)}) \neq 0$, supplied in the minimal TEP model by the disformal sector $B(\phi) \neq 0$.

2.2.1 Projected Disformal Contribution

To state the disformal contribution in a measurement-defined way, let $u^\mu$ denote the four-velocity field of the physical clock network or observer congruence, and let $P_\mu{}^\nu = \delta_\mu{}^\nu + u_\mu u^\nu$ be the spatial projector into the local rest space of that congruence. To leading order in the disformal correction, the projected contribution to the synchronization connection has the representative form (Jakarta, Appendix A3):

\begin{equation} \label{eq:disformal_sigma} \delta\tilde{\sigma}_\mu^{(\tau)} \simeq -\frac{B}{A^2}\,(u \cdot \nabla\phi)\,P_\mu{}^\nu\,\nabla_\nu\phi \end{equation}

up to the sign convention used for the synchronization one-form and higher-order disformal corrections. In a hypersurface-orthogonal $3+1$ slicing, where $u^\mu = n^\mu$, this reduces to:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:disformal_3plus1} \delta\tilde{\sigma}_i^{(\tau)} \simeq -\frac{B}{A^2 N}\,(\partial_i \phi)\,(n \cdot \partial\phi) \end{equation}

where $N$ is the lapse and $n^\mu$ is the unit normal to the spatial slices. Unlike $d\ln A$, the disformal contribution is not generically exact. Its curvature satisfies $d(\delta\tilde{\sigma}^{(\tau)}) \neq 0$ whenever the prefactor multiplying the projected scalar gradient varies independently around the loop.

2.2.2 Physical Condition for Non-Vanishing Holonomy

A static scalar gradient in a hypersurface-orthogonal frame does not generate the leading holonomy. The pulsar observable requires relative motion through the field, time dependence, mixed temporal-spatial structure, or independently varying lapse/shift and disformal response around the effective loop. The key factor is $u \cdot \nabla\phi \neq 0$: the four-velocity of the clock congruence must have a component along the scalar field gradient.

For the J0437−4715 scintillation geometry, $u \cdot \nabla\phi \neq 0$ is generated by three independent contributions:

  • Pulsar proper motion: J0437−4715 has a large transverse proper motion ($\mu_\alpha \simeq 121$ mas/yr, $\mu_\delta \simeq -71$ mas/yr; Deller et al. 2008), corresponding to a tangential velocity $v_\perp \simeq 104$ km/s at $d \simeq 156$ pc. The pulsar's worldline sweeps through the interstellar scalar field gradient, producing a time-varying $u \cdot \nabla\phi$ along the line of sight.
  • Earth's orbital motion: The Earth's orbital velocity ($v_{\rm orb} \simeq 30$ km/s) modulates the observer's four-velocity projection onto $\nabla\phi$ over annual timescales, producing a periodically varying contribution to $u \cdot \nabla\phi$.
  • Screen motion: The scattering screens at $\sim 89.8$ pc and $\sim 124$ pc have their own peculiar velocities relative to the local standard of rest, contributing additional temporal structure to the effective loop geometry.

These kinematic ingredients enter the theoretical prediction through the $u \cdot \nabla\phi$ factor in equations \eqref{eq:disformal_sigma} and \eqref{eq:disformal_3plus1}, not merely through the later Stokes-alignment algorithm. The predicted holonomy is therefore a function of the pulsar's proper motion, the Earth's orbital phase, and the screen kinematics — all of which are independently measured. The Stokes-aligned analysis (Section 4.7) is the observational implementation of this kinematic projection; the theoretical prediction precedes it.

Where the single-metric null hypothesis requires the closure residual to vanish, TEP predicts path-dependent residual synchronization holonomy as a consequence of disformal coupling in dilute environments where Temporal Shear remains unsuppressed. The Phase Closure $\psi$ provides a zero-centered test: $\psi = 0$ under the additive-delay null hypothesis, $\psi \neq 0$ for TEP.

This non-zero holonomy manifests with specific geometric properties. In a Stokes-aligned distribution, delays converge to a consistently negative mean ($-H$) when aligned with the orientation kinematics vector, while the magnitude $M_{\rm cl}$ remains invariant regardless of specific triplet geometry. The sign of the holonomy correlates with geometric orientation, defined by clockwise versus counter-clockwise configurations.

A critical consequence of this sign-geometry correlation is that simple aggregate averaging over all orientations yields $\langle \tau \rangle \approx 0$. This bipolar cancellation is expected by construction, not evidence against TEP. Analysis by orientation is therefore required to reveal the underlying signal, with the effect manifesting maximally in dilute environments where Temporal Shear is unsuppressed, such as the interstellar medium.

Key distinction: TEP predicts a bipolar signal where the holonomy sign flips with velocity projection. The primary detection statistic is the Phase Closure $\psi$, which represents the physical holonomy phase and is immune to noise-floor bias. The absolute magnitude $M_{\rm cl}$ is susceptible to Rice-distribution noise-floor bias: for zero-mean Gaussian noise with standard deviation $\sigma$, the folded-normal expectation is $E[|X|] = \sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}$. For J0437-4715 the per-epoch noise floor is quantified at $E[M_{\rm cl}] = 6.810$ ns, which saturates the raw unsigned mean ($8.100 \pm 0.102$ ns), leaving a noise-subtracted excess of $1.290 \pm 0.102$ ns ($t = 12.7\sigma$). The signed mean serves as a physical metric expected near zero due to bipolar cancellation, but the Phase Closure provides the primary test of TEP.

2.3 The ISM as an Unscreened Laboratory

Temporal Topology is the spatial and covariance structure of the conformal clock field \(\ln A(\phi)\), characterized locally by its gradients and statistically by \(C_A(x,x')\). Temporal Shear is the canonical synchronization gradient:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:temporal_shear} \Sigma_\mu \equiv \nabla_\mu \ln A(\phi) = \alpha(\phi)\,\nabla_\mu\phi . \end{equation}

The observable Temporal Shear is screened by the environmental operator \(\mathcal{S}_\Sigma(\mathcal{E})\):

\begin{equation} \label{eq:screening} \Sigma_\mu^{\rm obs} = \mathcal{S}_\Sigma(\mathcal{E})\,\Sigma_\mu , \end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{E}\) includes density, compactness, gradients, source structure, coherence scale and boundary conditions. Density is one environmental projection, not a universal switch. The disformal response is correlated with the same field configuration but is not fixed by \(\mathcal{S}_\Sigma\) alone. It depends additionally on \(B(\phi)\), the field solution, the observer congruence and the measurement channel.

The interstellar medium toward PSR J0437-4715 provides a suitable testbed for TEP effects. With typical densities $\rho \sim 10^{-24} \text{ g/cm}^3$, the ISM is a dilute environment. The scattering screens at $\sim 89.8$ pc and $\sim 124$ pc (Gwinn et al. 2006) create multiple path configurations that enable closure delay measurements around closed loops through the Galactic plasma. The diffuse ISM provides a long, low-compactness multipath baseline and is therefore a motivated candidate environment for an active J0437 response. However, $\rho \ll \rho_T$ alone does not determine $\mathcal{S}_\Sigma(\mathcal{E})$, nor does conformal unscreening by itself fix the disformal transfer. The J0437 analysis therefore constrains the channel-integrated non-exact response rather than inferring it from ambient density alone.

The anisotropic nature of scattering toward J0437-4715, documented by previous studies (Walker et al. 2004; Coles et al. 2011), naturally introduces preferred orientations that may affect the distribution of clockwise versus counter-clockwise triplet configurations. This ISM anisotropy does not invalidate the TEP test; rather, it provides a physical explanation for orientation asymmetries while preserving the core prediction of magnitude equality between orientations.

It is important to distinguish the unscreened ISM scattering screens from the pulsar's immediate binary environment. The white dwarf companion to J0437-4715 generates a deep gravitational potential that locally suppresses observable Temporal Shear through the environmental screening operator $\mathcal{S}_\Sigma(\mathcal{E})$. Within the companion's screening radius, the scalar field gradient flattens toward its minimum, attenuating the locally observable Temporal Shear. Consequently, orbital-scale kinematics are expected to be partially shielded, while proper-motion-scale kinematics—sampling the unscreened ISM at much larger distances—remain the dominant TEP channel.
